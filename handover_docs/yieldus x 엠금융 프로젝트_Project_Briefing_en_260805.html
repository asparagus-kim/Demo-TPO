1What we set out to build

In one sentence

A shop owner types their 10-digit business registration number and music that suits their store starts playing. We ask for nothing else.

Why music

Small merchants in Korea are a hard audience to reach with financial products. So we lead with something the owner actually wants, and the conversation that follows produces consultation data that goes to our partner, M Financial (엠금융).

Music is the hook; the business is the financial consultation. Get that order backwards and every downstream priority call comes out wrong.

Two uses for this demo

AudiencePurpose
CEO and M FinancialProve the concept holds up on real data before funding a full build
Door-to-door salesA kit: open a phone in front of an owner, type the number, play the music

Both uses imply the same requirement — it has to behave like a service that is really running. That is why nothing is precomputed and an analysis genuinely takes 2–10 seconds.

Out of scope

Considered and excluded. Please do not reopen these: AI music generation, the consultation form and CRM handoff, an admin console, multi-language UI, user accounts, payments.

2How we got here

In rough chronological order. Every turn had a reason, and without the reason you will be tempted to undo it.

Starting point — the previous developer's build

A FastAPI backend that took a business number and produced a store's atmosphere as three numbers: Energy, Tempo, Mood. The pipeline already covered tax-office status lookup, trade-area matching, Google Places, weather, and LLM review scoring.

The context that matters

At the time, no budget had been approved for paid APIs. So the business-number-to-company-name step was built by scraping the screen of a commercial vendor (moneypin) — a temporary implementation. That was not a bad decision. It was the first answer to a zero-budget constraint, and it got a working demo.

Turn 1 — Scraping to an official API

A scraped screen breaks whenever the target page changes, needs a headless browser inside the container, and is legally uncomfortable in a product we intend to sell. We moved to the official bizno REST API.

But the budget constraint did not change. We swapped an unofficial source for an official one at the same price: zero. And the bizno free tier returns only the company name.

Turn 2 — That single missing field reshaped the architecture

bizno free tier = company name only. No address. No industry. │ ▼ we cannot tell the Kakao search which region to look in │ ▼ stores with the same name become genuinely indistinguishable │ ├──▶ 1. score up to 15 Kakao candidates ├──▶ 2. a two-step screen where the user picks their store ├──▶ 3. entity validation reworked to run without an address └──▶ 4. infer a region hint from the branch name
The easiest thing to misread

Items 1–4 are temporary scaffolding. They exist because one field — the address — is missing. A paid lookup removes all four. Do not spend time polishing them.

Turn 3 — Google Places to Kakao Local product owner's proposal

The aim was to work around free-tier limits and get better coverage of Korean small merchants. Three reasons held up under measurement.

DimensionKakaoGoogle
Free quota100,000 / day1,000 / month
Industry signalRestaurant > Cafe > Coffee shop pathplace_types — blunt
Small-merchant listingsHigh — it is the map Koreans useLow

Why Google stayed: Kakao Local exposes no reviews and no opening hours at all, and review text carries 50% of the atmosphere score. Dropping it was not an option. Hence the deliberate two-map hybrid.

Turn 4 — Trade area: spreadsheet lookup to counting the neighbours

The old approach parsed a neighbourhood name out of the address and looked it up in a nationwide spreadsheet. It scored 37% on a 30-location benchmark.

Two bugs were involved — road-name addresses contain no neighbourhood at all, and lot-number addresses carry a legal neighbourhood while the spreadsheet is keyed by administrative neighbourhood. Fixing both left it at 37%, because those boundaries do not follow commercial reality.

We now classify by the ratio of business categories within a 500 m radius.

37% → 90%Trade-area accuracy (30 locations)
Coordinates onlyBehaves identically for any industry

Turn 5 — Music recommendation built from scratch

The earlier version produced the E/T/M numbers and stopped. The demo ended on a chart. The product promise is music, so a demo without music proves nothing. The recommendation engine is new.

Turn 6 — Two industries to eleven

The pilot scope was agreed with M Financial as cafes and gyms, so the track manifest covered exactly those. That was a scope decision, not an oversight.

What prompted the widening: a hair salon came through, matched no industry group, and got a cafe jazz track. Yet the source library already held 75 salon tracks, 173 bar tracks, 105 bookshop tracks — 604 of 664 tracks were sitting unused. The manifest was the bottleneck, not the library, and widening cost only curation time.

Moving to the paid API is not an improvement item — it is the original plan resuming. Only budget approval is outstanding. The switch deletes roughly 280 lines of code and removes an entire screen from the user's path.

2. The store-picker screen is not a bug

Measured against 10 real businesses: 5 resolved automatically, 5 required the user to choose.

Fitness World has stores in Jung-gu and Jongno-gu with identical names and different owners. Both candidates score 1.00 : 1.00. No algorithm separates identical strings. Only an address can.

Expect this screen more often in industries full of common names — pharmacies, convenience stores, clinics.

3. The current UI is placeholder work

Layout, colours, copy and information placement are all due for a full redesign. None of it is a specification. Do not work to preserve it.

What does need preserving is not the screen but the API response schema and its three states — normal, needs-user-input, and error. Any new UI must handle all three.

4. Reviews are capped at 5 per store — money cannot lift this

It is a Google Places API limit, not a tier limit. Yet review text carries 50% of the atmosphere score. That is thin evidence, and it is the biggest quality risk in the current design. Discussion 2 covers it.

5. Track metadata is maintained in a spreadsheet

Each track's atmosphere data — BPM, energy, mood, suitable industries, suitable time slots and so on — is maintained in an internal Google Sheet and reaches the service along this path.

Google Sheet source of truth, maintained by hand │ export ▼ data.js 664 tracks · canonical in the player project │ scripts/curate_demo_tracks.py ▼ data/demo_tracks.json 620 tracks · the manifest this service reads │ ▼ runtime matching no external calls

Fields actually used for matching: bpm · energy_level · valence · arousal · suitable_industries · suitable_time_slots · customer_density_fit · purpose_tags · genre · mood · vocal_type

Fields excluded: warmth, sophistication and quality_score (populated on very few tracks); suitable_weather (identical on every track, so it carries no signal).

The manifest embeds the metadata it needs, so nothing at runtime reads the spreadsheet or the player project folder. After adding tracks or editing metadata, re-run the curation script to regenerate the manifest.

4What the system does

The ten-step pipeline

1 Validate 10-digit business number 2 Tax office active / suspended / closed → stop here if closed 3 Company name bizno → if it fails, ask the user to type it 4 Match store Kakao keyword search → industry, address, coordinates └ ambiguous? show the candidate list and stop 5 Trade area count business categories within 500 m 6 Reviews/hours Google Places (max 5 reviews per store) 6.1 Identity is the Google place the same store as the Kakao one? 5.6 Supplement no reviews or hours → fill in from web search 7 Weather current observation (coordinates → grid conversion) 8 LLM scoring review text → energy / tempo / mood signals 9 Fusion LLM + trade area + industry + time of day + weather → final E/T/M 10 Music pick 10 from a local 620-track manifest (no API call)

6Discussion 1 — how do we make music matching more accurate?

Here is how it works today and where it falls short, followed by the directions I have in mind. If you have a better idea, we should take it.

How tracks are chosen now

The store's E/T/M scores become a target, we compute the distance from each candidate track, and take the 10 closest. Weights: energy 30%, tempo 25%, mood 20%, time of day 15%, crowding 10%.

The key design choice — pool-relative normalisation

Track metadata and store scores are on incompatible scales.

valence spans only 0.65–0.85 → absolute comparison ignores mood entirely energy_level gym tracks are nearly all 8.0 → constant; no absolute scale separates them bpm cafe 60–129 / gym 118–160 → the distributions barely overlap

So each axis is min-max normalised within the candidate pool and compared as a relative position. Narrow axes regain discriminating power, and an axis whose values are constant automatically becomes "no information" and drops out of the score.

Three measured limitations

Limitation 1 — almost no discrimination within the fitness industry

Of the 62 gym tracks, nearly all are EDM at energy_level 8. Raising the target energy from 60 to 75 leaves 8 of the top 10 unchanged.

In practice a busy 24-hour gym and a quiet neighbourhood gym get almost the same playlist. Cafes, now at 284 tracks, overlap only 3–5 of 10 between adjacent settings.

The cause is the track metadata, not the algorithm.

Limitation 2 — not enough time-of-day tagging

Only 104 of the 664 source tracks carry a lunch tag. Some industry groups have no lunchtime coverage at all, and adding more tracks will not fix it.

Limitation 3 — a full listening pass has not been done yet

A listening check across all 620 tracks is still ahead of us. However accurate the algorithm is, a track that does not suit the room halves the effect — so working through a sample per industry group, one group at a time, seems the sensible way in. For reference, 191 tracks have vocals.

Directions I propose — open for discussion

PriorityDirectionWhat it means, and the expected gain
High Rework the track metadata Re-tag energy, time of day and crowding for gym tracks against their real distribution — morning workout, low-intensity stretching, yoga. Needs the music team, and would help more than any algorithm change.
High Human QC Listen through and mark unsuitable tracks review: reject. Can be done incrementally, and it is the surest way to avoid an embarrassing moment on a sales call.
Medium A feedback loop Let owners mark a track good or bad and feed that back into the weights. Today the flow is one-way. What the UI for this should be is worth discussing.
Medium Re-recommend by time of day We analyse once. A real store feels different in the morning, at lunch and in the evening; giving one store several time-based playlists is the natural next step.
To decide The reinforcement-learning code The repository contains 2,235 lines of PPO experimentation that learns prior weights. Nothing in production calls it and it has never been evaluated. We should decide together: keep, finish, or delete.

7Discussion 2 — how do we make store analysis more accurate?

Accuracy is at stake in three places

Place 1 — identifying the store (the biggest problem)

With no address, we search by name alone. 5 of 10 test businesses needed the user to choose. In industries full of common names — pharmacies, convenience stores — it gets worse.

The fix is clear: the paid API. Algorithms have a ceiling here; two stores with genuinely identical names cannot be told apart by name.

An interim mitigation worth discussing: ask the owner only for their district — a single word. It narrows candidates sharply and is less effort to type than the business number itself.

Place 2 — the evidence behind the atmosphere score is thin

Five reviews carry 50% of the score, and some stores have none at all (measured: Fitness World returned zero).

The web-search supplement currently runs only when reviews are missing. Making it run always would thicken the evidence — but web-search quality is uneven, so what confidence rule we blend it under is the open question.

Also worth evaluating: Naver Place reviews (far more numerous for Korean small merchants), Instagram hashtags, delivery-app reviews.

Place 3 — trade area (solved, but the benchmark is small)

We took it from 37% to 90%. But the benchmark is 30 locations and the expected labels are my own judgement; there is no objective ground truth for commercial district types.

Whether the same thresholds hold outside the capital region is untested — the current benchmark is Seoul-heavy.
